Auditing the auditors

How Much Bias You Find Depends on How You Ask

We held a meal's ingredients byte for byte identical, changed only how its culture was signalled, and collected roughly 260,000 healthiness ratings from twenty open language models. Telling the models the culture outright found almost no shared bias. Letting the culture ride in with a dish name found four times more. Moving the same sentence below the ingredient list found five times more. The audit was measuring itself.

A question you can ask a language model 260,000 times

Ask a language model to rate how healthy a meal is, on a scale of one to ten, and it answers without complaint. People do this constantly: dietary advice is one of the highest-volume consumer uses of these models. So a natural worry follows. If the model quietly rates food from some cultures as less healthy than identical food from others, that prejudice flows straight into everyday advice, aimed at exactly the populations that nutrition guidance already serves worst.

The standard way to check is a bias audit, and the recipe sounds airtight. Take a meal. Hold its substance fixed. Change only the cultural marker. If the score moves, you have caught the model discriminating between two things that differ only in culture.

This study, from Vizuara Research, ran exactly that audit at scale: six cuisines, eighteen ingredient lists, twenty open-weight instruction models from eleven different families, three instruction phrasings, three ingredient orderings, and every combination of the above, roughly 260,000 generations in all. But it was not built to measure which cuisines get marked down. It was built to measure the audit.

The choice hiding inside every audit

Here is the detail that most audits never report. The cultural marker has to be delivered, and ordinary language offers several equally natural deliveries. You can name the people: this is a Japanese recipe. You can name the country: this recipe is from Japan. Or you can say nothing about culture at all and simply name the dish, Miso Shiru, and let the culture ride in quietly with the name.

These feel interchangeable. They are the same fact, wrapped three ways. Every audit picks one wrapper, usually without comment, and reports its result as the model's bias.

Two audits of the same twenty models, both internally consistent, both correct about their own measurements, detected amounts of shared bias that differ by a factor of four, because one said the culture aloud and the other let a dish name imply it.

Eighteen meals that never change

Everything in the design exists to make one sentence true: whatever moves the rating, it cannot be the food.

Each of the eighteen items is an ingredient list built on a fixed template: a grain, a protein, a vegetable, a fat, an aromatic, a seasoning. The lists are asserted byte-identical across conditions by the harness itself, not matched by human judgment. Two more rules are enforced programmatically: no ingredient string contains a country word or a demonym, so the list cannot leak culture on its own, and every list is vegetarian and alcohol-free, so religious taboos never collide with a cuisine label. Prior work on food knowledge compared real dishes, which tangles cultural signal with genuine nutritional difference; here the confound is removed by construction rather than by statistics.

One subtlety matters enough to flag. When the signalled cuisine matches the ingredients' own provenance, the model is judging a plausible pairing, which measures something closer to coherence than culture. Those congruent cells are excluded from the main measurement and analysed separately; the congruence effect turns out not to explain the channel gap, and it nearly vanishes for invented names, which have no canonical dish to clash with.

An ethics note the paper states plainly and this article repeats: nutrition is held constant by construction, so nothing here says, or could say, that any real cuisine is healthier than any other. Every number below is a measurement of model behaviour toward identical food. No ranking of cuisines is published, here or in the paper.

Twenty judges, and one honest yardstick

What should an audit actually report? A single model's gap on a single cuisine is a shaky object: greedy decoding makes each rating deterministic, but change the phrasing or the order of ingredients and the number wobbles. The study measures that wobble directly, by rating every item under nine nuisance variations, and finds a noise floor of half a rating point. Any single-model effect smaller than that is weather, not climate.

So the estimand is agreement. For each model, compute its cuisine effect: how much the lead sentence for cuisine c shifts its rating of the same item under a neutral lead, averaged over items whose ingredients do not come from c. That gives each model a six-number vector, one per cuisine. Then ask: do the twenty models rank cuisines the same way? The agreement score, written ρ below, is the average correlation between every pair of model vectors. A bias one model shows and nineteen do not is a quirk of that model. A bias they share is a property of the class, and that is what an audit is trying to detect.

Say the culture outright, and the models barely agree

Deliver the signal explicitly, this is a Japanese recipe, and the twenty models agree on which cuisines to mark down at ρ = +0.089. That is not nothing: with twenty models it clears significance at p = 0.017. But it is small enough that an auditor eyeballing the same result at a more typical audit size would call it a null. Name the country instead of the people and agreement drops to +0.050, which does not clear significance at all.

Hand the models a real dish name instead, and agreement jumps to ρ = +0.365, four times the demonym figure. Both measurements are pre-registered, both use the same models, the same eighteen ingredient lists, the same rating scale, the same slot in the same prompt. And a difference between a significant and a non-significant result is not itself a significant difference, so the paper tests the contrast directly: the gap is +0.276, with a 95% confidence interval of [+0.03, +0.46] and p = 0.0125.

Name the dish, and they agree four times over

Why would a name carry more measurable bias than a plain statement of the same fact? One reading is mundane: alignment training teaches models to be careful when a prompt says Japanese or Nigerian out loud, and that care flattens whatever cultural association would otherwise move the score. The signal is not gone; it is suppressed at exactly the moment the audit looks for it. A name slips past that guard, because nothing in Cassoulet announces that culture is being tested.

But there is an obvious rival explanation, and it is the strongest objection to the whole result. A dish name does not just signal a culture. It names a dish, a real one, with its own typical preparation and its own nutrition profile. If every model happens to know that Miso Shiru is a light soup and Cassoulet is heavy, models would agree with each other about dishes, factually and correctly, and the audit would read that shared knowledge as shared cultural bias. Removing one name at a time cannot settle this, because all five names within a cuisine share the same confound.

Dishes that do not exist

So the study builds thirty dishes that do not exist. Each invented name is assembled from a cuisine's productive naming morphology, the sounds and word-shapes its real dish names use: Ruvellata al Forno, Habuki-jiru, Obe Ajiri, Sopa Zarquena. To a reader they carry an unmistakable cultural accent. But they denote nothing. There is no recipe to remember, no canonical preparation, no nutrition prior to look up.

A manipulation check confirms the accent transmits: shown only an invented name and asked where the dish is from, models recover the intended cuisine at 75.9% accuracy against a 16.7% chance floor. Real names transmit at 92.7%.

If shared dish knowledge were driving the agreement, invented names should collapse it. They do not. Agreement under invented names is ρ = +0.350, statistically indistinguishable from the +0.365 of real names (p = 0.44). Stability checks point the same way: drop any one name and the invented-name ordering barely moves, while real names wobble more, exactly as expected if real names carry dish-specific information that invented ones cannot.

It is not knowledge about food. A name that denotes nothing, but sounds like a cuisine, carries the full bias signal.

Move the sentence, and the audit sees five times more

The second discovery was stranger, and the paper found it while checking something else. Two ablations asked whether the weak explicit result was an artifact of the measurement apparatus. First, the rating scale: maybe one to ten is too coarse to register small explicit effects. Rerunning on a 0 to 100 scale reproduces the main result almost exactly, +0.131 explicit versus +0.370 implicit, so coarseness is not the story.

Then, position. The lead sentence had always come first, before the ingredients. Move the very same sentence to after the ingredient list, and the explicit channel's agreement rises from +0.089 to +0.493, more than fivefold. The implicit channel rises too, from +0.365 to +0.535. And the cuisine ordering under the moved sentence correlates at rank r = +0.87 with the original, so this is not a different bias conjured by a different prompt. It is the same bias, measured far more sensitively.

Why would recency matter this much? A plausible mechanism is attention: the last thing before the question sits closest to the generation and is hardest for the model to discount. But the paper is careful not to over-claim a mechanism. What it claims, and demonstrates, is the measurement fact: detectable bias is a property of where the signal sits, as much as of what it says.

Two audits that would have lied

Two of the study's supporting results deserve their own section, because each one is a complete, self-contained audit failure that produced a beautiful looking number.

The flawless, entirely spurious null

Forced choice sounds like the sharper instrument: show two meals with identical ingredients, differing only in the cultural label, and make the model pick the healthier one. Run it, and the result looks like a textbook null: win rates of exactly 0.5, cuisine after cuisine. The truth is that 17 of the 20 models are position-degenerate. They pick the first option, or the second, almost regardless of content, and because the presentation order is counterbalanced, their win rates are forced to exactly one half by construction. The instrument is not reporting an unbiased model. It is reporting its own symmetry. Only three models survive the degeneracy screen, far too few to conclude anything.

The model that said too much

An earlier version of the pipeline capped generation at 8 new tokens. One model liked to begin, I’d rate this meal as an, and the cap cut it off right there, before the digit. Every one of its ratings failed to parse; the correlation code silently dropped the all-empty column; and the model was still counted in the study's headline total. Raising the cap to 24 tokens recovered the same model, finishing its sentence, as an 8. The moral is small but sharp: a generation cap is a silent selection criterion on which models your audit contains, and parse rates are a result to report, not plumbing.

Honest about scale

Everything above concerns whether the ordering of cuisine effects is shared across models. None of it says the effects are large, and the paper goes out of its way to keep the two claims separate.

The magnitudes: spreads between cuisine means run from 0.12 to 0.33 rating points, on a one to ten scale, against a measured noise floor of 0.50. Only 7 of 120 model-cuisine cells clear the pre-registered smallest effect size of interest. This is a reproducible ordering of small effects, and the paper says so in those words, declining to claim that real-world harm follows at this size.

And a result about the audit's own power, reported with unusual candor: an earlier version of this study used 13 models. At that size, the name-versus-demonym contrast came out at +0.270 with p = 0.081, an interval spanning zero. Same effect, same design, not enough models: statistical power around 0.53, a coin flip. At 20 models the power reaches 0.76 and the contrast lands. The study that discovered the channel effect could not have detected it one design iteration earlier.

A single-channel audit measures a joint property of the model and the channel, and reports it as a property of the model.

That sentence is the paper's real contribution. The recommendations that fall out of it are concrete: hold content identical rather than matched; report at least one explicit and one implicit channel; vary the signal's position, not only its wording; baseline against a neutral marker rather than an absent one; verify the signal actually transmits; audit parse rates; screen forced-choice designs for position degeneracy; and use enough model families for the contrast you care about to be detectable at all.

Where this sits in prior work

Bias in model-as-judge settings is well documented: judges favor certain positions, longer answers, and their own outputs, and writing style shifts automated grading. Cultural food knowledge specifically has been probed by comparing real dishes across cuisines, which measures knowledge and bias together; this study removes the food by construction. On the measurement side, a growing thread shows that delivery mechanics move results at fixed semantics: serialisation format changes tool-calling accuracy, superficial code style changes code judgment, and offering an abstention option changes behaviour where abstention is unwarranted. Multilingual judging work has reported lower-resourced categories scored more generously, the same direction found here under explicit labels.

What was already known

  • LLM judges carry position, verbosity, and self-preference biases
  • Models rate real dishes from different cuisines differently, with food and culture entangled
  • Prompt format can move measured capability at fixed meaning
  • Single-judge findings are fragile; recent work evaluates many judges

What this study adds

  • Nutrition held identical by construction, so only the cultural signal varies
  • The signalling channel itself changes detected bias fourfold, tested directly
  • Invented-name control separates cultural form from dish knowledge
  • Signal position changes detected bias fivefold while preserving the ordering
  • A 13-model version of the same study could not detect its own effect

What this does not show

  • Six cuisines, eighteen items, thirty real and thirty invented names, one judgment dimension, one prompt family, open-weight models only. The channel effect is demonstrated in this box, not beyond it.
  • The effects are small. Most model-cuisine cells sit under the pre-registered smallest effect of interest, and the paper explicitly does not claim real-world harm at this magnitude.
  • Invented names transmit cuisine less strongly than real ones (75.9% vs 92.7%), which may itself depress the invented-name estimate. The control is conservative with respect to the paper's claim, but not free of that asymmetry.
  • The manipulation check is model-based, not human-normed: it shows the invented names are legible to models, which is the relevant quantity here, but not that they carry the same associations for people.
  • Five of 25 candidate models were excluded for parse rate, and verbose models may differ systematically from concise ones.
  • The healthiness construct is thin. A rating out of ten may track richness, unfamiliarity, or perceived processing; ingredients are fixed, so whatever it tracks is being applied to identical food, but the study cannot say which construct is reported.
  • Nothing here ranks real cuisines by healthiness, and the design makes such a reading impossible: the food never changes.

Appendix: the statistics from scratch

Four tools carry the whole paper. None needs more than a paragraph.

The agreement score ρ

Each model gets a vector of six numbers: its average rating shift for each cuisine's signal, on non-congruent items, relative to a neutral lead sentence of the same shape. Take every pair of models, correlate their two vectors, and average the 190 correlations. That average is ρ. It is deliberately scale-free: a model that reacts strongly and one that reacts faintly can still agree perfectly on the ordering, and ordering is what a class-level bias claim is about. The price of that invariance is that ρ says nothing about size, which is why the magnitudes get their own section.

The permutation test

Is ρ = +0.365 large? Compared to what? Shuffle each model's six cuisine effects independently, so every model keeps its own numbers but any shared ordering is destroyed, and recompute ρ. Do this 5,000 times and you have the distribution of agreement that pure coincidence produces. The fraction of shuffles beating the observed value is the p-value. This assumes nothing about which ordering models would agree on, only that under the null there is none.

Testing the contrast directly

The study's key claim is not that one channel is significant and another is not; that comparison is a classic fallacy, since significance is a threshold and thresholds create false cliffs. The claim is that the difference between channels is itself significant. For each model, swap its two channel vectors at random and recompute the gap 10,000 times; the observed +0.276 sits outside what swapping produces. A bootstrap over models gives the confidence interval, resampling the unit that generalises: if you care about models as a class, you resample models, not individual ratings, because nine prompts about one dish are not nine independent facts.

Reliability, and the attenuation trap

A subtle alternative story: implicit cells average five names while explicit cells have one, so implicit effect vectors are less noisy, and noisier vectors correlate worse. Classical measurement theory has a name and a correction for this, disattenuation by split-half reliability. After correction the implicit advantage survives (+0.416 versus +0.125), and pinning the implicit channel to a single name, matching explicit precision exactly, still leaves it far ahead. The channel gap is not a reliability artifact.

Reproduce it

The audit needs no GPUs beyond what runs the models, and the analysis is a few hundred lines. The paper releases code, prompts, stimuli, the exclusion list, and all raw ratings.

stimuli/     18 ingredient lists, byte-identical across conditions
             30 real dish names, 30 invented names (5 per cuisine each)
prompts/     4 channel lead sentences + neutral lead, in one shared slot
             3 instruction paraphrases x 3 ingredient orderings
run/         20 open-weight instruction models, 11 families
             greedy decoding, 24-token generation cap (see parse audit)
analysis/    per-model cuisine-effect vectors on non-congruent items
             rho = mean pairwise correlation; permutation B = 5000
             direct channel contrasts: exchange B = 10^4 + model bootstrap
             reliability, congruence, magnitude, power analyses

The headline numbers to check against, all from the paper: channel agreement +0.089 / +0.050 / +0.365 / +0.350; the direct contrast +0.276 [+0.03, +0.46], p = 0.0125; the invented-versus-real difference +0.015, p = 0.44; position moves +0.089 to +0.493 and +0.365 to +0.535 with rank r = +0.87; noise floor 0.500; power 0.53 at 13 models and 0.76 at 20. The full details are in the paper PDF.
